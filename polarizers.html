Through a Polarizer

Two polarizing filters, crossed at a right angle, block every last photon — the classic doorway that lets nothing through. Now slide a third filter in between them, tilted at some other angle, and watch light reappear on the far side. Nothing was hidden behind the first two; each filter is a measurement, and the basis you measure in changes what survives. That is the whole content of "measurement basis," made visible.

What you're seeing: Polarizer A fixes the incoming light's polarization at 0° — call that the input state. Polarizer C is fixed at 90°, crossed with A, so with nothing between them the transmitted intensity is cos²(90°) = 0. Insert a filter at angle θ between them and two things happen in sequence: it passes cos²(θ) of the light through (measuring the beam in its own basis, at angle θ, and only keeping the "yes" outcome), and what emerges is now polarized along θ, not along 0° — so Polarizer C sees a beam already rotated toward it, and passes cos²(90°−θ) of that. Multiply the two: cos²(θ)·sin²(θ), maximized at θ=45°, where a full quarter of the original light gets through a gap that was, a moment ago, completely dark. The single-photon mode makes the same law probabilistic: each photon independently survives each filter with exactly that probability, and, over enough photons, the tally converges on the readout above it. Nothing was smuggled through — the middle filter's measurement, in its own basis, is what let it in.
